Compared to state-of-the-art local learning meth-\nods that require observing post-synaptic neuronal outputs to optimise\nsynaptic weights via error-based gradient descent, Forward Projection\noperates under a stricter constraint,ﬁtting weights using just the pre-\nsynaptic neuronal activity and labels.\nThe target generation function proposed here for Forward Pro-\njection promotes input and label encoding in neural membrane\npotentials (see section S.8 in SI). Joint encoding of labels with pre-\nsynaptic activity alleviates the degenerate neural activity which results\nfrom local modelling of simple label projections, whilst avoiding the\nintroduction of uninformative noise (see section S.6 in SI). In this\nanalysis, a simple non-linearity was employed for target generation.\nThe utility of more complex nonlinearities for target generation is a\nsubject for further research.\nExplainability of neural network models is an important limitation\nin decision-critical ﬁelds such as biomedicine, where errors such as\nconfounded decisions may lead to signi ﬁcant consequences\n35.F P -\ntrained layers may be interpreted without downstream information,\nproviding insight into model reasoning in hidden layers. FP inter-\npretation yielded informative outputs in three model architectures,\nidentifying clinically salient features in ECG sequences and OCT ima-\nges. An important advantage of FP training is that saliency maps may\nbe generated before downstream ﬁtting, permitting on-the- ﬂy\ninspection of intermediate layers for suf ﬁciency or confounding.\nThus, expert scrutiny of hidden layer performance may be conducted\neven before the downstream architecture isﬁnalised.\nFew-shot learning is ubiquitous in biological systems, which\nexhibit rapid neuronal adaptation to changing environments\n36.I no u r\nexperiments, Forward Projection demonstrated clear performance\nadvantages over backpropagation and local learning approaches in\nfew-shot learning tasks, presenting a plausible method for learning\nnew tasks rapidly. In this setting, convolutional features learned by\nForward Projection yielded more generalisable models than back-\npropagation, which overﬁtted in some experiments and underﬁtted in\nothers, even underperforming random features in some cases. For-\nward Projection also maintained reasonable performance on activa-\ntion functions that were untrainable by SGD-based methods (see\ns e c t i o nS . 1 4i nS I ) .\nThe closed-form Forward Projectionﬁti sc o m p u t a b l ei nas i n g l e\npass over the data for each layer, presenting an opportunity to\nexpedite training and reduce environmental footprint. The efﬁciency\nof FP training is attributable to a substantially different operational\nsequence from that employed in SGD-based approaches. Firstly, FP\ntraining collects a Gram matrix of pre-synaptic activity over a single\nepoch, which is subsequently inverted during a one-step weight matrix\ncomputation. Secondly, FP completes ﬁtting for each layer before\ninitialising successive layers. In contrast, SGD-based approachesﬁta l l\nthe layers in the network iteratively, using feedback from neuronal\noutputs and downstream layers. FP training requires no retrograde\ncommunication between neural output activations and presynaptic\nconnections, enabling direct training on hardware with unidirectional\nsynaptic and neuronal communication. This differs from current\niterative local learning methods, which require backward commu-\nnication from neuronal outputs to optimise pre-synaptic parameters\n(see section S.2 in SI). Although FP addresses the feedback-free con-\nstraint inherent in biological learning systems, the biological plausi-\nbility of one-step learning via matrix inversion remains uncertain.\nIterative ﬁtting via gradient descent in sequential, batch-based learn-\ning offers a practical and scalable approach for aligning weights with\nlocally generated targets, as de ﬁned in ( 1) (see section S.11 in SI).\nAlthough closed-form optimisation remains a challenge for recurrent\narchitectures, future research will explore integrating iterative training\nwith random temporal convolutions to generalise the FP framework to\ndynamic models and expand its applicability to temporal learning\ntasks. This backpropagation-free strategy holds promise for biologi-\ncally inspired computing systems, such as spiking neural networks,\nwhere non-differentiable activation functions preclude standard\nbackpropagation\n37–39. FP training may be applicable as a pre-training\nstep to reduce the number of training epochs required for back-\npropagation. In conclusion, FP is an ef ﬁcient approach for neural\nnetwork optimisation, employing techniques from randomised pro-\njective embedding and linear regression to ﬁtw e i g h tm a t r i c e si no n e\nepoch using a single-step solution.
